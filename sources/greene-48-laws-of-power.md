# The 48 Laws of Power (Robert Greene, 1998)

**Covers:** attention and distribution, proof over persuasion, isolation risk, negotiation leverage, reading buyer motives, defense against manipulation. **Use for:** judging how an idea gets noticed, how it gets sold and negotiated, and whether its founder builds alone; spotting the levers someone pulls on the founder. Use it as a pattern-recognition tool, never as a code of conduct.
**Provenance:** web research summary of the book, filtered to the 10 laws usable by an entrepreneur, with 5 more flagged to ignore; synced 2026-10-09.

## Laws

### G48-1 Law 9: Win through your actions, never through argument 🟠
- **Rule:** When an idea needs someone to believe in it (a buyer, a partner, an investor), show them a result they can check instead of arguing, because people discount promises and trust proof: one paying customer persuades more than a pitch deck.
- **Violation tells:** the plan to convince rests on decks, documents or debates; weeks of planning documents and no buyer conversation; objections answered with longer explanations.
- **Cheapest check:** replace the next argument with something observable (a recorded demo, a pilot result, a first payment) and count who acts after seeing it.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 9. Field reading: 50 calls to prospects are worth more than 50 planning documents.

### G48-2 Law 6: Court attention at all costs 🟠
- **Rule:** When judging an idea, ask how it will get seen before asking how good it is, because without attention the best product dies unknown: distribution comes before product, and visibility is oxygen.
- **Violation tells:** no named channel; a launch plan that amounts to "publish and they will come"; product polish months ahead of any audience; no visibility metric tracked.
- **Cheapest check:** put the idea in front of the target audience through one channel (a post, a landing page, direct messages) before building, and measure replies, sign-ups or clicks.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 6.

### G48-3 Law 18: Do not build fortresses to protect yourself: isolation is dangerous 🟠
- **Rule:** When a founder plans long stretches of solo work, require a social counterweight (buyers, peers, a reviewer who disagrees), because isolation breeds burnout and self-confirmation bias and cuts off the information that would correct the plan.
- **Violation tells:** a solo sprint of 60-hour weeks with no outside review; no customer contact for weeks; decisions checked only against the founder's own notes or against tools that agree with them.
- **Cheapest check:** count the conversations with people outside the project since the last major decision; zero means the fortress is up.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 18; the brain note rates it the most applicable law for a solo founder.

### G48-4 Law 28: Enter action with boldness 🟠
- **Rule:** When stuck between two options, choose one and commit fully, because hesitation loses both the upside of the bold choice and the safety of the cautious one.
- **Violation tells:** two plans kept alive in parallel; a decision reopened every few days; half-effort spread across both options.
- **Cheapest check:** set a decision date, choose, write down the reason, and judge the outcome against that reason instead of re-deciding midway.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 28.

### G48-5 Law 13: When asking for help, appeal to people's self-interest, never to their mercy or gratitude 🟠
- **Rule:** When selling or asking for anything, state what the other side gains, because people act on their own interest and what the seller deserves moves no one. Defensively: a buyer asking for a "friend price" appeals to generosity; decline and restate the value.
- **Violation tells:** copy about the founder's effort or story instead of the buyer's gain; requests framed as favors; discounts granted out of sympathy.
- **Cheapest check:** reread the pitch and strike every sentence that does not name a gain for the buyer; send the cut version.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 13.

### G48-6 Law 33: Discover each man's thumbscrew 🟠
- **Rule:** When selling, find the lever that moves this buyer (fear, vanity, need for recognition, need to belong) before pitching, because modern selling comes down to identifying the right lever. Defensively: name the lever someone presses on you when they sell to you.
- **Violation tells:** one generic pitch for every buyer; no discovery questions before the offer; the founder cannot say what the buyer fears or wants.
- **Cheapest check:** in the next five buyer conversations, ask what worries them most and note the lever before making any offer.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 33.

### G48-7 Law 4: Always say less than necessary 🟠
- **Rule:** When negotiating or pitching, say less than you could, because every extra word hands the other side leverage; discretion creates power.
- **Violation tells:** volunteering a floor price, a deadline or the lack of alternatives; over-explaining after an objection; filling every silence.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 4.

### G48-8 Law 1: Never outshine the master 🟠
- **Rule:** When pitching a senior investor or an established partner, let them shine instead of proving them wrong, because a bruised ego kills deals that logic would win.
- **Violation tells:** the pitch corrects the partner's past views; the founder competes to look like the smartest person in the room.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 1.

### G48-9 Law 3: Conceal your intentions 🟠
- **Rule:** When a move can be copied or countered, reveal it once executed, because announcing "launch in three months" gives competitors three months to prepare.
- **Violation tells:** a public roadmap of unreleased competitive moves; announcements made long before shipping.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 3. Radical transparency has worked as an advantage for some companies (see Limits), so weigh this law only for competitive moves.

### G48-10 Law 17: Keep others in suspended terror: cultivate an air of unpredictability 🟠
- **Rule:** When facing competitors, avoid full predictability, in small doses, because a predictable player is easy to counter.
- **Violation tells:** pricing, launches and promotions follow a pattern that competitors and buyers can time.
- **Source:** The 48 Laws of Power, Law 17; the brain note insists on dosing it.

## Thresholds and numbers
- The book gives no quantitative thresholds: its laws are historical patterns, not measured effects (🟠, brain note).
- 10 of the 48 laws judged usable by an entrepreneur without ethical compromise, 5 flagged to ignore; about 30% of the book applies to a founder, the rest is court-specific, toxic, or better covered by The Laws of Human Nature (🟠, brain note verdict).

## Anti-patterns
The first five are the laws to ignore. Greene justifies them by history; transposed to modern business they produce toxic and sometimes illegal behavior (fraud, harassment, manipulation).
- **Law 7: Get others to do the work for you, but always take the credit:** claiming collaborators' work -> toxic in modern collaboration; it burns the trust a small team runs on.
- **Law 14: Pose as a friend, work as a spy:** friendship used as an intelligence channel -> destroys authentic relationships.
- **Law 21: Play a sucker to catch a sucker: seem dumber than your mark:** feigned naivety to exploit someone -> pure manipulation.
- **Law 27: Play on people's need to believe to create a cultlike following:** a product or brand run as a cult -> ethically repugnant.
- **Law 38: Think as you like but behave like others:** permanent public conformity -> the founder loses integrity.
- **Power as a way of life:** every interaction read as a power game -> paranoia, loss of trust and intimacy; psychologists link living by these laws with narcissism and pathological mistrust.

## Limits
- Assumed amorality: Greene does not judge royal-court behavior, and transposing it to business without a filter yields toxic or illegal conduct.
- Paranoia risk: read literally, the book shows manipulation everywhere and erodes trust and healthy relationships.
- Historical cherry-picking: examples chosen to confirm each law, counter-examples ignored; many people succeeded while breaking these laws.
- No proven causality: "a ruler did X and won" does not show that X caused the win.
- Outdated context: a royal court is not a startup. Radical transparency and authentic leadership have become competitive advantages for some companies, the opposite of several laws.
- Cultural fit: most useful in strongly hierarchical settings (politics, large corporations), less in egalitarian startup teams built on co-creation.
- Greene's defense: the laws describe how power has been used, they do not prescribe; refusing them is a choice, ignoring them leaves you exposed to those who use them.
- For constructive psychology, prefer The Laws of Human Nature (GHN), the same author's more mature book.
