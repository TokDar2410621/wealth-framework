# Principles: Life and Work (Ray Dalio, 2017)

**Covers:** the goal-to-result loop, root-cause diagnosis, design flaw versus execution failure, learning from pain, believability-weighted advice, ego and blind spots. **Use for:** ordering any decision (goal, problem, diagnosis, plan, execution), reading a failed test before killing or keeping an idea, and weighing conflicting advice. It says little about offers and selling.
**Provenance:** research summary built from 17 secondary sources that corroborate each other; only the author's official site was checked live and the book's table of contents was not verified, so every law here stays 🟠; synced 2026-10-09.

## Laws

### DAL-1 Embrace reality and deal with it 🟠
- **Rule:** When reality contradicts the plan, stop wishing it were different and decide from what is, because results come from big dreams combined with radical acceptance of reality and determination; a plan that needs the market to behave as hoped fails on contact.
- **Violation tells:** disconfirming data explained away; the plan depends on buyers acting differently than they did in every test so far; "it will work once people understand it".
- **Cheapest check:** write the three facts the plan must survive (who pays, how much, through which channel) and mark each as observed or hoped.
- **Source:** Principles, Life Principles (secondary summaries).

### DAL-2 Accurately diagnose the problems 🟠
- **Rule:** When a problem shows up, find its root cause before choosing a fix, separating the symptom from the disease, because most diagnosis failures come from treating symptoms, and a fix aimed at a symptom lets the problem return.
- **Violation tells:** a solution proposed in the same breath as the problem; the fix targets the visible effect (traffic, churn, silence) without a stated cause; the same problem recurs after each fix.
- **Cheapest check:** ask "why" until the answer names something you can change, and write that cause down before any fix.
- **Source:** Principles, step 3 of the 5-Step Process (secondary summaries).

### DAL-3 Your machine: designer and worker 🟠
- **Rule:** When a result misses its goal, step out of the worker role (who executes) into the designer role (who observes and redesigns), compare outcomes with goals, and ask whether the machine has a design flaw or suffered an execution failure, because each calls for a different fix (see "Design or execution?").
- **Violation tells:** the founder judges the idea while still inside the execution; a failed test ends in "the idea is bad" or "I must try harder" without asking which; the machine never gets redesigned, only pushed harder.
- **Cheapest check:** after each test, one line: "design", "execution" or "void test", with the evidence for it.
- **Source:** Principles, the machine metaphor (secondary summaries).

### DAL-4 Pain + reflection = progress 🟠
- **Rule:** When something hurts (a failed test, a critique, an exposed weakness), reflect once the emotion has cooled, extract the lesson, write it as a principle and apply it, because pain signals something important to learn; a mistake is acceptable, failing to learn from it is not.
- **Violation tells:** the same mistake repeated; failures blamed on others or on luck; no written lesson after a failed launch; the post-mortem done in the heat of the moment, or never.
- **Cheapest check:** after each failure, write what happened, why, and the rule that would have prevented it.
- **Source:** Principles (secondary summaries). The author's own case: a wrong call on a depression in 1982 nearly ruined his firm.

### DAL-5 Believability-weighted decision making 🟠
- **Rule:** When weighing advice, weight each opinion by the adviser's proven record on this type of problem (repeated successes) and by their ability to explain the cause-effect chain behind those successes, never by rank or volume, because opinions differ in value. When a believable person disagrees, ask yourself: "How do I know I'm right?"
- **Violation tells:** advice taken from someone with no record on this problem; all opinions averaged; deference to the most senior or the loudest voice; a believable dissenter dismissed without hearing the reasoning.
- **Cheapest check:** next to each adviser, write how many times they solved this exact problem and whether they can explain why it worked.
- **Source:** Principles, Work Principles (secondary summaries). The success threshold is 🔴, see Thresholds.

### DAL-6 Radical open-mindedness: the ego barrier and the blind-spot barrier 🟠
- **Rule:** When you feel sure of a decision, seek out smart people who disagree and understand their reasoning before acting, because the ego barrier makes you want to be right more than to see what is true, and the blind-spot barrier means everyone sees reality through their own lens.
- **Violation tells:** feedback sought only from people who agree; defensiveness or excuses under critique; no record of anyone credible having tried to break the plan.
- **Cheapest check:** before committing, put the plan in front of one credible person likely to disagree and write down their strongest objection.
- **Source:** Principles, Life Principles (secondary summaries).

### DAL-7 Write your principles down 🟠
- **Rule:** When the same kind of decision comes back, write the criteria used, then revise and prune them as outcomes come in, because situations repeat and explicit principles make the next decision faster, more consistent and less emotional; pushed further, they become algorithms that decide alongside you.
- **Violation tells:** the same debate rerun from scratch for each similar decision; criteria that live only in the founder's head; no record of why past decisions were made.
- **Cheapest check:** for the next recurring decision, write the rule used in one sentence and check it against the outcome later.
- **Source:** Principles (secondary summaries).

### DAL-8 Idea meritocracy 🟠
- **Rule:** When a team decides, let the best idea win wherever it comes from, through radical truth, radical transparency and believability-weighted decision making, because both autocracy (the boss decides) and democracy (one voice, one vote) ignore who is likely to be right.
- **Violation tells:** disagreement voiced in private and never in the room; decisions taken by title; information withheld to win arguments.
- **Source:** Principles, Work Principles (secondary summaries). Weigh it against the culture critiques in Limits.

### DAL-9 Meaningful work and meaningful relationships 🟠
- **Rule:** When setting a goal, aim at meaningful work done with people you value, because the author names that as the end goal, ahead of money.
- **Violation tells:** a goal stated only as a revenue figure; work chosen with people the founder does not want to work with.
- **Source:** Principles (secondary summaries).

## The 5-Step loop (filter skeleton)
Dalio's 5-Step Process (🟠): a good life repeats five steps, again and again, the way evolution adapts or dies. Run any idea through them in order; when it fails, name the step where it breaks. Each cycle brings new information, so the loop is a spiral: raise the load each round, like weight training, then aim higher. Know the step where you personally tend to fail and reinforce that weak link.

1. **Have clear goals.**
   - **Ask:** What do I actually want, given who I am?
   - **Typical failure:** aiming without self-knowledge; a fuzzy goal, which leaves every later step without direction.
2. **Identify and don't tolerate the problems.**
   - **Ask:** Which problem stands between me and the goal?
   - **Typical failure:** not looking at the problem because seeing it clearly is uncomfortable; tolerating a known problem.
3. **Accurately diagnose the problems.**
   - **Ask:** Symptom or root cause? Design flaw or execution failure?
   - **Typical failure:** jumping to solutions and treating the symptom.
4. **Design a plan.**
   - **Ask:** Which tasks take me from the problem to the goal?
   - **Typical failure:** a plan that stays implicit, never written as tasks.
5. **Push through to completion.**
   - **Ask:** Did I execute until there was a result?
   - **Typical failure:** stopping before the result; ignoring your own weak link.

## Design or execution?
After any failure, Dalio asks from the designer's seat: was the machine badly designed, or was a good design badly executed? The summaries give the question without a procedure (the missing "how" is a known weakness of the book), so the checklist below is an operational reading of it, 🔴 to test.

1. **Audit execution first.** List the tasks the plan required and mark each one: done in full, partly, late, or not at all. Execution is cheaper to check and is the most common confound.
2. **Incomplete execution means a void test.** No verdict on the design: the test did not run. A plan that called for 100 prospect messages, of which 15 went out, says nothing about the offer. Fix the execution and rerun the same test before changing anything.
3. **Full execution with a missed goal means a design flaw.** Trace the chain from task to goal and find the assumption that broke: wrong buyer, wrong pain, wrong channel, wrong price, wrong mechanism. Return to step 3 of the loop, then step 4.
4. **Signs of a design flaw:** the miss repeats across faithful attempts or across different people running the plan; buyers saw and understood the offer, then declined; the expected cause-effect link never shows up even at full volume.
5. **Signs of an execution failure:** tasks skipped, late or half done; the miss traces to one step that did not happen; buyers never actually saw the offer; a faithful rerun, or someone else running the same plan, gets the result.
6. **Repeated execution failure is a design flaw one level up.** When the same step keeps failing (the selling part never gets done), the machine was designed around a worker who will not do that task: redesign the role, the person or the plan, and reinforce the weak link.
7. **Both at once:** fix execution first, because only a faithful run can test the design.

Run this diagnosis once the emotion of the failure has cooled (DAL-4), and write the verdict down: "design", "execution" or "void test".

## Thresholds and numbers
- Believability: roughly three documented successes on the same type of problem, plus a cause-effect explanation of them (🔴 unverified: comes from secondary summaries, not checked against the book).
- Idea meritocracy = radical truth + radical transparency + believability-weighted decision making (🟠, secondary summaries).
- About a quarter of new hires at the author's firm reportedly leave within 18 months (🔴, critical secondary sources).

## Anti-patterns
- **Verdict from a void test:** an idea declared dead (or proven) after a test that was never fully executed -> the decision rests on noise, mixing execution and design.
- **Symptom fixing:** a fix aimed at the visible effect -> the root cause stays and the problem returns.
- **Tolerated problem:** a known problem left alone because facing it is uncomfortable -> the step where most people fail, according to the author.
- **Implicit plan:** tasks kept in the head -> nothing to execute against, nothing to audit afterward.
- **Being right over being accurate:** critique met with excuses or blame -> the ego barrier blocks the lesson.
- **Averaging opinions:** every voice weighted the same, or the senior voice wins -> believable dissent gets lost.
- **Transplanting the hedge fund culture:** recorded meetings and public ratings imposed on a small team -> critics report surveillance and public humiliation, and the culture exports poorly.

## Limits
- Overall 🟠: a summary of 17 secondary sources; only the official site was checked live; the book's table of contents was not verified; the believability threshold is 🔴.
- Culture described as cult-like: former employees report stress and fear of being fired; The Fund (Rob Copeland, 2023) argues that radical transparency produced surveillance and public humiliation.
- Possibly self-justifying: the financial press calls the principles sound but not new, even banal; the book says little about investing, the author's actual trade, which feeds the suspicion of a post-hoc rationalization of a success also due to luck and context.
- Vague implementation: the "how" is often missing, and the culture exports poorly outside a highly competitive hedge fund.
- Says little about offers and selling: it complements offer and validation sources (HOR, DEM), it does not replace them.
