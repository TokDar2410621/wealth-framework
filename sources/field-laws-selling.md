# Field laws: selling and offers (Darius, 2026)

**Covers:** offer framing and copy, pricing signal, risk reversal and guarantees, persuasion ethics, proof and trust, asking and referral, commitment devices, cold-contact legality in Canada. **Use for:** judging an offer, a price, a guarantee, a pitch or a page; judging whether a sales ask (testimonial, referral, upsell, first offer) is designed to go out; judging whether a cold-contact plan is legal and timely.
**Provenance:** field synthesis from a solo founder's research log (100+ sessions on why people buy, with the offer playbook, the decision rules and the knowledge notes distilled from it), plus the studies those notes cite; synced 2026-10-09.

## Laws

### FLS-1 Sell a solution, never a product 🟠
- **Rule:** When writing any offer, page, pitch or outreach message, open on the pain, desire or status the buyer already has, in the buyer's words, and bring in the product only as proof that it solves it; when the buyer does not yet know a solution exists, name the pain before any solution (awareness stage before the formula), because buyers buy outcomes and will not do the work of translating a feature into a benefit for themselves.
- **Violation tells:** the headline names the technology or a feature; "our platform enables companies to..."; a raw metric as the first line ("mobile load time: 20 s") instead of what it does to the buyer ("a customer searching on a phone waits 20 seconds, then calls your competitor"); a copy formula (AIDA, PAS) picked before the buyer's awareness stage is known; a solo builder describing what was built instead of what the buyer wants solved or wants to become.
- **Cheapest check:** the 3-question test on the first sentence, before sending: (1) does "you" appear in the first sentence? (2) is the benefit a concrete lived scene (an immediate mental image), not a metric or an abstraction? (3) would a hurried prospect see what they gain with no effort of imagination? One "no" means rewrite. Keep the technical diagnosis as an attachment and the message in personal language.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): the rule "sell a solution to a pain, a desire or a status, never a product"; personal vs impersonal positioning (a marketing book page, title not recorded: a positioning works best when the prospect has no effort of imagination to make); Schwartz's five awareness levels (Breakthrough Advertising); concrete language (Packard & Berger, Journal of Consumer Research).

### FLS-2 Reverse the risk onto the seller 🟠
- **Rule:** When an offer asks a buyer to pay before seeing the result, move the risk to the seller with a guarantee that is specific, triggered automatically, written against a stated price and tied to an observable result (a continuation guarantee beats a vague money-back promise), and judge a calculated seller-side risk (pay-for-performance, "you pay only for the results you confirm as real") as an engine to bound, not a danger to warn about, because perceived risk is the main brake on a purchase and a credible guarantee removes it, while a vague or unpriced one removes nothing.
- **Violation tells:** no guarantee at all; "satisfaction guaranteed" with no trigger; a refund promise on a price that was never written down; an unconditional guarantee on a usage-billed product (invites abuse, sounds desperate); a guarantee the seller cannot keep; a review that titles a pay-for-performance offer "the real danger" instead of bounding it. In a category associated with scams, a bigger guarantee reads as one more promise: show proof instead (FLS-4).
- **Cheapest check:** score the guarantee against Hart's criteria (unconditional in spirit, easy to understand, meaningful, easy to invoke, easy to collect, credible) plus one from the playbook (sustainable for the seller); write the price next to it; bound the seller's downside in time ("a failure costs two months, not the company").
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): offer playbook, risk-reversal section; Hart, "The Power of Unconditional Service Guarantees", Harvard Business Review, 1988; specific and automatic beats unconditional (rated probable by the brain: Chen; Gremler et al., Journal of Service Research). Field, observed once: a written promise to "refund the whole month" on a price never stated reduced no perceived risk (field law: a badly written guarantee is worse than none). Rule from the maintainer: a calculated risk that threatens neither life, health, solvency nor an irreversible commitment is the engine, not a problem.

### FLS-3 Never position as "cheaper" 🟠
- **Rule:** When positioning or pricing, compete on "guaranteed, proven, simpler", never on a low price, and choose the billing unit (the value metric) before the number, because price signals quality (an abnormally low price triggers suspicion), a price-led differentiator draws the buyers who churn and haggle, and margin per customer beats volume for a small team.
- **Violation tells:** "the cheaper alternative to X"; a launch price set low "to break in"; a price built as cost plus margin; tiers separated by features instead of by client result; a survey or a price-sensitivity questionnaire treated as willingness to pay; charm or decoy pricing debated before the unit is chosen; a unit that does not grow with the value delivered.
- **Cheapest check:** quote the target price to real buyers and ask for a payment (a deposit, a pre-order): only a payment reveals willingness to pay. Test a new price on new buyers, never by re-pricing existing ones. Check the unit against four criteria: grows with the value, predictable for the buyer, hard to game, aligned with the cost of delivery.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): marketing placebo effect, the same product priced higher is experienced as better (Plassmann, O'Doherty, Shiv & Rangel 2008, PNAS; Shiv, Carmon & Ariely 2005, Journal of Marketing Research); the field law "a price-led differentiator brings the wrong cohort" rests on literature and was never tested in the field; core law 8 (only a payment reveals willingness to pay).

### FLS-4 Lived proof beats promised proof 🟠
- **Rule:** When an offer needs to be believed, let the buyer live the result (a demo on their own case, a free first result, an onboarding that reaches the first moment of value) and publish only verifiable, operational trust signals, because value lived first-hand is the strongest proof, and trust sells by removing the reason to say no rather than adding a reason to say yes; in a category associated with scams, prove and show, never promise bigger.
- **Violation tells:** testimonials, logos and adjectives instead of a demo; activation defined as setup completed (profile filled) rather than the first concrete result; trust theater (a badge or claim nobody can verify); a meeting with slides and no live demonstration; "no demand" concluded without checking that the link is not flagged by a browser, a search engine or a spam filter.
- **Cheapest check:** measure the time from first contact to the buyer's first concrete result (target minutes, not days); before concluding "no demand", check the domain's security status and email deliverability.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): core law 2 (recurring in 8+ sessions); trust as reduction of operational risk for B2B and developer buyers (versioning, status page, documentation, data policy, clear business model); positioning in a discredited category (move the buyer's question from "will it work?" to "can I see it work?"). Field, observed once: a third-party security flag on a product's domain went unnoticed for weeks while the founder read the silence as missing demand.

### FLS-5 Kill inertia: no decision is the first competitor 🟠
- **Rule:** When selling to a decider who fears getting it wrong, make a firm recommendation with a reduced scope, add an explicit safety net, offer a tiny first step with no commitment, and close every meeting on a dated, concrete next step (a trial, a pilot installation), because most lost deals die to "no decision", fear of messing up beats fear of missing out, and piling on benefits raises the anxiety. Frame the sale by the real axes: number of people who must say yes (one vs a committee) x rhythm of the need (continuous vs one-off) x the decider's personal risk, never by the B2B or B2C label.
- **Violation tells:** a meeting that ends on "thanks, any questions?"; a first step that requires a contract, a migration or a committee; more features added to overcome hesitation; strategy derived from the label "B2B" or "B2C"; a self-serve motion pushed on a market that buys done-for-you.
- **Cheapest check:** write the smallest reversible step the buyer can take this week, offer only that, and count how many prospects take it.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): The JOLT Effect (Dixon & McKenna, from about 2.5 million recorded sales calls); CEB, Google and Motista survey of about 3,000 B2B buyers (avoiding blame outweighs functional benefits); core law 4 (the label hides the mechanism: a developer tool bought by one person on a card acts like B2C, a one-off high-stakes household decision acts like B2B). Field, observed once: a solo founder's self-serve product drew no outside buyer across dozens of sessions while a done-for-you service from the same founder closed on its first delivered deal: the selling mode must match how the market buys.

### FLS-6 Honest signals sell: admit one limit, real scarcity only 🟢
- **Rule:** When writing a sales page or message, state one real, minor limit linked to the main strength, and use urgency or scarcity only when it is true (real capacity, real deadline), because two-sided messages raise credibility, perfection sets off the buyer's scam detector, and fake timers and false scarcity are deceptive practices that destroy trust. Across the field synthesis every manipulative shortcut had a net negative cost (core law 1: ethics equals efficacy).
- **Violation tells:** a page with no weakness and perfect 5.0 ratings; a countdown that resets on refresh; "only 3 left" with no real constraint; fake or borrowed social proof; fear audits used as bait; astroturfing; hidden fees; an exit wall that makes cancelling hard; unverifiable trust badges.
- **Cheapest check:** for each urgency claim, name the real constraint behind it; if there is none, cut the claim. Add one limit sentence ("nobody can guarantee X; here is what we measure instead") and compare replies with and without it.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Eisend 2006, meta-analysis of two-sided messages; the US Federal Trade Commission's position on fake countdown timers and false scarcity as deceptive dark patterns; Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern) on review ratings; core law 1, confirmed four times in the synthesis (sourced fear with a solution converted better than fear-mongering; the legal channel was also the most effective; proof given upfront beat a fear audit; genuine community participation was the only durable entry). In a category of scammers, openly admitting the limit they hide works as an anti-guarantee.

### FLS-7 The perception filter 🟠
- **Rule:** When shaping any offer or piece of copy, ask "which perception does this move?" and deliver the minimum real substance that produces the maximum felt change, through a visible structural lever (proof, demo, guarantee, less friction, price), never through a cosmetic trick and never through a lie, because a problem has an objective layer and a felt layer, people pay to move the felt one, it is far cheaper to move, and micro-nudges show near-zero effect once publication bias is removed. Perception without substance churns; substance without perception stays invisible.
- **Violation tells:** a costly objective improvement the buyer never sees; copy that lists improvements without changing how the problem feels; anchoring tricks, decoys or button-color tests run before friction, proof and price are fixed; a felt gain the product does not deliver (churn follows).
- **Cheapest check:** before adopting a tactic, ask whether it changes the targeted need, the perceived risk, the friction, the proof, the price or the availability; if none, skip it. Show the deliverable to one buyer and ask what changed for them.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): decision "perception is the lever" (3 of the 4 terms of Hormozi's Value Equation are already perceptions; guarantees and social proof are pure perception levers); core law 3 (structural over cosmetic); Maier et al. 2022, PNAS (nudge effects vanish after correcting for publication bias); Baymard Institute on checkout friction.

### FLS-8 Fear only with a credible solution 🟢
- **Rule:** When selling prevention (a purchase made to avoid a loss), put the threat and a simple, credible, reachable solution in the same sentence, with a concrete, sourced and personal loss, because fear appeals work only when paired with efficacy statements: fear alone produces denial, avoidance and reactance, and a loss weighs about twice an equal gain.
- **Violation tells:** dread with no exit; inflated or unsourced loss figures; abstract "peace of mind" in a first contact; the solution introduced paragraphs after the threat.
- **Cheapest check:** check that every threat sentence carries its solution and a source; ask a buyer to compute the loss with their own numbers.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Witte's Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM); Tannenbaum et al. 2015, Psychological Bulletin (meta-analysis, 127 papers, 248 samples; effect stronger with efficacy statements, a severe threat and a one-time act); loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky). Synthesis rule (🟠): the prevention purchase fires when three conditions coexist: a concrete loss that can happen to this buyer, a serious one, and a simple, credible solution; remove the solution and the buyer tips into denial.

### FLS-9 Askers underestimate the yes 🟢
- **Rule:** When a warm ask is ready (testimonial, referral, upsell, a first offer to a known contact) and does not go out, treat the hesitation as a calibration error and fix it mechanically: ask orally at the next live contact, easiest ask first, several asks batched into one decision, anchored on a neutral event (an invoice, a delivery), with a firm deadline; then accept a no at no cost to the other person, because requesters underestimate compliance by about half: they weigh what the yes costs the other person and miss the social cost of saying no.
- **Violation tells:** an ask rewritten many times and never sent; warm, named asks postponed while cold or self-contained tasks go out; the most important ask sent by email to a stranger; the reverse abuse: pressing in-person asks that exploit the discomfort of refusing.
- **Cheapest check:** make one ask orally this week and record the answer; compare the number of asks you predicted for one yes with the number it took.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Flynn & Bohns, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (14,000+ real requests in total; askers expected to need about 7 people for one yes, it took about 2); Bohns 2016, Current Directions in Psychological Science; Roghanizad & Bohns 2017, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology (with strangers, an in-person ask beat email by about 34 times, and email askers overestimated their persuasiveness). Field, the most robust law of the log (execution follows emotional cost): dozens of recommended warm, named asks never went out while cold or self-contained tasks did.

### FLS-10 A referral ask raises the giver's loyalty 🟢
- **Rule:** When a recent customer has paid and lived the value, ask for a testimonial and an introduction (orally first, written follow-up within the hour), and tell a referred customer honestly that they came by referral, because making a recommendation lowers the recommender's own defection, referred customers are worth more and churn less, and referred customers refer more: the perceived relational cost of asking has the wrong sign. Proven in B2C programs; the transfer of the mechanism to small B2B relationship asks is probable and untested in the field.
- **Violation tells:** no referral ask after a successful delivery; the ask deferred until the relationship "matures" (the effect is strongest on recent relationships); more than about one ask per customer per quarter (over-solicitation reverses the effect); the loyalty effect used as an argument ("this will make you more loyal"), which turns the ask into manipulation.
- **Cheapest check:** one testimonial-plus-introduction ask to the most recent paying customer, anchored on the invoice or the delivery.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Garnefeld, Eggert, Helm & Tax 2013, Journal of Marketing (field experiment with a telecom operator); Schmitt, Skiera & Van den Bulte 2011, Journal of Marketing (about 10,000 bank customers over 3 years); Gershon & Jiang 2025, Journal of Marketing Research (41.2 million customers plus a field experiment). Field status: never tested, no referral ask left the log.

### FLS-11 A commitment device needs a date, a witness and a stake 🟠
- **Rule:** When the content is good, the channel chosen and the message written but the action still does not leave, treat the bottleneck as behavioral and bind it: a dated deadline, a named witness who receives proof, and a real consequence (a small financial stake, a public pledge); the same mechanism works on the buyer side, where a call to action with no deadline and no real stake lets the sale slip. Remove any one of the three and the effect falls toward nothing.
- **Violation tells:** an if-then plan stored in a note with no cue and no witness; "soon" instead of a date; a recurring self-report that nobody outside reads; the same blockage diagnosed again instead of changing the system; a call to action with no date.
- **Cheapest check:** a one-line contract: "If X is not sent by [date, time], I pay [amount] to [named witness], with proof." If it stays blocked, cut the batch (one message instead of four).
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Matthews 2007, Dominican University (written goals, action commitments and a weekly report to a friend: 76% achievement vs 43% for goals only thought about); Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002 (deadlines reduce procrastination). Flags carried from the brain: the Matthews study was not published in a peer-reviewed journal, the Ariely result is reported as not replicated, and the law was never tested in the field (the contract was never set up). Field, observed: private if-then plans kept in a file moved none of the actions they targeted.

### FLS-12 Cold contact in Canada: the channel sets the law 🟢
- **Rule:** When contacting a cold prospect in Canada, call a published phone number first (phone calls fall outside CASL), send an email, SMS or direct message only under consent (implied when the person published the address without a "no solicitation" statement and the message concerns their business role, express otherwise), skip anyone who posted "no solicitation", keep a record of how consent arose, and time the contact on a public trigger event (a new listing, a job posting, a launch), because the legal channel is also the cleanest one and the trigger turns a cold contact into a timely one (the size of the trigger effect is 🔴).
- **Violation tells:** a bought list blasted by email; a message unrelated to the recipient's role; no record of the consent basis; volume preferred to relevance (in a local market, reputation is the asset and a blast burns it); a pitch before any discovery questions; follow-ups that ask "did you see my message?" instead of adding value.
- **Cheapest check:** before the first touch, write next to each contact its channel and its consent basis (published number; published address plus role-relevant message; express consent). No basis, no message.
- **Source:** Field synthesis (Darius's research sessions): Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL: no exemption for a cold B2B email; implied consent through a published address) and the CRTC Unsolicited Telecommunications Rules for calls (public law); trigger-based outreach and founder-led discovery before the pitch (industry practice). Ten targeted contacts beat a hundred blasts.

## Thresholds and numbers
- Meta-law of the synthesis: win by reducing the real risk and proving lived value, at the right moment, for the right decider, at the right price and payer, in the right cultural register, with no manipulative shortcut (🟠, field synthesis).
- Requesters underestimate the chance of a yes by about 48% (🟢, Flynn & Bohns).
- With strangers, an in-person ask is about 34 times more effective than email; with existing customers the gap narrows but the order holds (🟢 on strangers, directional otherwise; Roghanizad & Bohns 2017).
- Making a recommendation cut the recommender's yearly defection from 19% to 7% and raised their monthly revenue by 11.4% (🟢 in its B2C context, Garnefeld et al. 2013; not a prediction for another business).
- A referred customer is worth 16 to 25% more and churns about 18% less (🟢 in its B2C context, Schmitt et al. 2011).
- Referred customers make 31 to 57% more referrals; honestly reminding them they came by referral adds 21% (🟢, Gershon & Jiang 2025).
- At most about one testimonial or referral ask per customer per quarter (🟠, brain note guardrail).
- Fear appeals: d = 0.29 over N = 27,372 (🟢, Tannenbaum et al. 2015).
- A loss weighs about twice an equal gain (🟢, Kahneman & Tversky).
- 40 to 60% of lost B2B deals go to no decision (🟠, Dixon & McKenna, The JOLT Effect).
- Displaying reviews: up to +270% purchase likelihood, most of it from the first 5 reviews; 4.2 to 4.5 stars convert better than a perfect 5.0 (🟢 as rated by the brain note, Spiegel Research Center).
- Concrete language: +9% satisfaction and +13 to 30% spending over 90 days (🟢, Packard & Berger, Journal of Consumer Research).
- Nudges: d ≈ 0.004 after publication-bias correction (🟢, contested, Maier et al. 2022).
- Cart abandonment about 70%; up to +35% conversion from better checkout design; surprise costs cause 48% of abandonments, forced account creation 26% (🟢 as rated by the brain note, Baymard Institute).
- Structured accountability (date + witness + consequence): 76% goal achievement vs 43% (🟠, flagged: Matthews 2007, not peer-reviewed).
- For a small team, 100 customers at $50 beat 500 at $10: same revenue, about 5 times less support, lower churn (🟠, field arithmetic).
- Van Westendorp price surveys systematically underestimate the acceptable price (🟠, brain note).
- Requiring a card for a trial cuts 60 to 70% of sign-ups (🔴, secondary benchmark).
- Median activation is about 25%: three quarters of sign-ups never see the value (🔴, secondary benchmark).
- Trigger-based outreach: about 2 to 3% meetings cold, 6 to 10% or more with a trigger (🔴, sales blogs with survivor bias).
- Over 70% of B2B buyers slow a deal when security information is not quickly verifiable (🔴, vendor sources with commercial bias).
- After a trigger contact: day 0, a value-adding follow-up at day 2 or 3, an open-door message at day 6 or 7, then stop; at most 2 follow-ups (🟠, field note).

## Anti-patterns
- **Feature-first copy:** the message describes the product, the system or the company -> the buyer has to translate it into a benefit, does not recognize their problem, and does not buy.
- **"The cheaper alternative":** low price as the differentiator -> signals low quality, attracts the buyers who churn and haggle, and leaves no margin for service.
- **Unpriced or vague guarantee:** "refund the whole month" with no price written, "satisfaction guaranteed" with no trigger -> removes no perceived risk and reads as a trap or as desperation; worse than no guarantee.
- **Manipulative shortcuts:** fake timers, false scarcity, fake or borrowed reviews, astroturfing, fear audits, hidden fees, exit walls, trust theater -> deceptive in the regulator's eyes, and a net negative cost once trust is lost.
- **Orphan fear:** a threat with no solution in sight -> denial and inaction instead of a purchase.
- **Nudge tinkering:** anchoring, decoys and color tests before friction, proof and price are fixed -> effects near zero once publication bias is removed.
- **Benefit pile-up against hesitation:** answering a stalled buyer with more features -> raises the fear of getting it wrong and feeds "no decision".
- **The simulated no:** a warm ask kept in drafts because the refusal feels likely -> the refusal is about half as likely as it feels, and a referral ask would have strengthened the relationship.
- **The plan in a file:** an if-then plan or self-report nobody else sees -> vague accountability, near-zero effect.
- **Warning against a calculated risk:** flagging a pay-for-performance offer or a result guarantee as the main danger -> feeds the founder's "not ready yet" block instead of bounding the risk and moving.
- **The blast list:** cold emails to a bought list -> breaks CASL in Canada and burns the local reputation that a small seller lives on.

## Limits
- No buyer-side law in this file has been validated in the field log: across 60+ execution sessions, the log validated laws about the seller and the instruments only (no reply and no sale was caused by a recommendation). The buyer laws rest on the cited literature.
- The asking and referral numbers come from strangers or from B2C programs, often with rewards; the brain rates their transfer to small B2B asks as probable and their exact amplitudes as uncertain.
- The commitment-device literature is weakened (Matthews not peer-reviewed, Ariely and Wertenbroch reported as not replicated), and the device was never tried in the field.
- The publication-bias correction behind "nudges ≈ 0" is itself contested; defaults and some nudge types may keep real effects.
- Spiegel Research Center and Baymard Institute figures come from research-institute reports, not peer-reviewed journals; the brain rates them proven.
- The perception filter rests on illustrative cases, not measurements; the claim that the felt layer costs 10 to 100 times less to move is unaudited (🔴).
- "Specific guarantees beat unconditional ones" is rated probable, not proven. It also pulls against FLS-4: in a category associated with scams, any guarantee can read as one more promise; there, lead with proof and a specific measurement guarantee.
- The CASL summary is not legal advice and is Canada-only. The brain note does not cover CASL content requirements (sender identification, unsubscribe mechanism) or the CRTC telemarketing obligations that still apply to calls; check the current texts before a campaign.
- Several benchmark numbers (activation, card-required trials, trust-center gains, trigger lift) come from secondary or vendor sources and are directional only.
- The synthesis was built on North American small-business and developer markets; the brain notes that the emotional engine depends on the category (prevention buyers run on loss aversion) and the tone depends on the geography, so the register has to be recalibrated for another market.
