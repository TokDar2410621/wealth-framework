# The Art of the Start: The Time-Tested, Battle-Hardened Guide for Anyone Starting Anything (Guy Kawasaki, 2004)

**Covers:** making meaning, the mantra, market sizing in a pitch, pitch decks for prospects and partners, personal vs impersonal positioning. **Use for:** judging a venture's reason to exist, a market-size claim, a sales or partner deck, and the first lines of outreach or landing copy.
**Provenance:** reading notes from photographed pages of the French edition (*L'art du démarrage*): p. 24 (meaning, mantra) and pp. 84-85 (market sizing); pp. 79-80 (pitch structure) come from a book the brain note only tentatively identifies as this one; one page on positioning comes from a book the brain note does not name. ## Laws

### KAW-1 Total target market 🟠
- **Rule:** When a pitch needs a market size, give the size of the segment you can actually reach, built bottom-up, instead of an analyst's industry total, because a small, precise number proves you understand your market and lends credibility to the rest of the pitch, while an inflated number destroys it.
- **Violation tells:** three or four market-size slides quoting analyst firms; a total near $50 billion; a 4 to 5 year horizon (close enough to sound credible, far enough to be unverifiable); "if we get 1% of this market".
- **Cheapest check:** rebuild the number as reachable buyers in the target area x realistic annual spend on this category. The book's example, paraphrased: a new restaurant counts what people spend on its cuisine within a drivable radius of the site, not national dining-out spend.
- **Source:** The Art of the Start, chapter on pitching (French edition pp. 84-85, where the term is MTC, *marché total cible*).

### KAW-2 Spark the imagination 🟠
- **Rule:** When the product's usefulness is obvious, drop the market studies and give a short chain of facts that lets the audience compute the market themselves, because a number the listener builds belongs to them, while an imposed number triggers distrust. The author's example, paraphrased: a tool that tests website input fields for security holes (nearly every site accepts typed text; there are vast numbers of sites; every company fears being hacked; so a great many companies need it).
- **Violation tells:** the market is proven by citation rather than by reasoning the listener can redo; the chain rests on the delivered mechanism (a technical score, an architecture) rather than on the customer's outcome.
- **Cheapest check:** say the chain aloud to someone outside the field; if they reach "so a lot of people need this" unprompted, it works.
- **Source:** The Art of the Start, chapter on pitching (French edition pp. 84-85, heading *suscitez l'imagination*; the English heading is not verified). The author warns that the method fails when the market is not obvious. The corollary "outcome, not mechanism" is the brain note's, not the author's.

### KAW-3 Make meaning 🟠
- **Rule:** When defining why a venture exists, complete the sentence: if this organization never existed, the world would be worse off because... A venture with no concrete answer lacks a reason to exist, because great companies grow from the desire to make meaning. Meaning does not guarantee success, but a failure in its pursuit was still worth the effort.
- **Violation tells:** the only answer is money; the answer would fit any competitor; the answer lists features.
- **Cheapest check:** write the sentence and read it to one target customer; a shrug means rework.
- **Source:** The Art of the Start, French edition p. 24.

### KAW-4 Make mantra 🟠
- **Rule:** When writing the company's "why", compress it into a short internal mantra about the meaning the company creates for its customers, and keep it apart from slogans and mission statements, because the mantra is the team's own answer to "why do we exist", not a line for marketing.
- **Violation tells:** a long mission statement; a tagline written for ads; buzzwords that could fit any company; a mantra that describes the product instead of the meaning.
- **Cheapest check:** ask each founder or team member to say in five words, without notes, why the company exists; diverging answers mean there is no mantra yet.
- **Source:** The Art of the Start, French edition p. 24 (mantra length: see Thresholds).

### KAW-5 Personal vs impersonal positioning 🟠
- **Rule:** When writing positioning, outreach or landing copy, state the benefit in personal terms ("you" as subject or beneficiary, a concrete lived result, the specific over the general), because the prospect then needs no effort of imagination to see which need the product meets. Paraphrased from the page: "shrinking the ozone hole" becomes "protects you from skin cancer"; "raising the school's average" becomes "your child will learn to read". Personal phrasing also tends to be shorter and simpler.
- **Violation tells:** the first sentence starts "our solution enables companies to..."; the benefit is a metric or a technical score; macro abstractions ("industry standard", "dozens of planes nationwide"); the reader must translate a feature into a gain.
- **Cheapest check:** three questions before sending (the brain note's test, not the book's): is "you" in the first sentence? Is the benefit a concrete lived image rather than a metric or an abstraction? Would a hurried prospect see what they gain without effort? One "no" means rewrite.
- **Source:** one photographed page of a French-translated marketing and positioning book. The brain note does not name the book; this law is grouped with the Kawasaki notes, not attributed to him.

### KAW-6 Prospect pitch 🟠
- **Rule:** When pitching a prospect, open with a title slide that says in one plain sentence what the organization does ("we sell software"), then the customer's problem, then an outline of the solution with no technical deep dive; continue with the business model (what you sell, its value, the names of customers who already bought), the underlying magic (few words, mostly figures and diagrams), a demo, the competition (KAW-9), the team (to make the prospect comfortable working with a startup) and next steps (KAW-7). Keep it to about ten slides in a twenty-minute meeting, with discussion after each slide, because the prospect must grasp at once what you do and why it matters to them.
- **Violation tells:** a title slide that never says what the company does; the problem framed as the seller's need; a technical lecture on the solution slide; text-heavy slides; a monologue.
- **Cheapest check:** show only the title slide to someone outside the company; they must say what the company does in one sentence.
- **Source:** pp. 79-80 (chapter III) of a photographed French-edition book; the brain note says the content strongly matches The Art of the Start but the attribution is still to confirm.

### KAW-7 Demo and next step 🟠
- **Rule:** When a live demonstration is possible, show it instead of more slides, and end every pitch with a concrete call to action (a trial period, a test installation), because a good demo is worth a thousand slides and a pitch with no next step ends with nothing decided.
- **Violation tells:** a deck with no demo for a product that exists; a closing slide that says "thank you, any questions?".
- **Cheapest check:** before the meeting, write down the exact next step you will ask for.
- **Source:** pp. 79-80 (attribution to confirm); the "never close on questions" phrasing is the brain note's synthesis.

### KAW-8 Partner pitch: 2 + 2 = 5 🟠
- **Rule:** When pitching a potential partner, first check that the partner sells (or wants to sell) to the same customer, keep the problem slide on that end customer's problem, turn the solution slide into the synergy (how the two together serve the customer better than apart) and the business model slide into a partnership model (who does what, when, how and why), because a partner joins for a combined result greater than the sum, not to meet your needs.
- **Violation tells:** pitching your own need to the partner; no shared customer; a vague division of work.
- **Cheapest check:** name the customer both organizations already sell to; if there is none, do not pitch the partnership yet.
- **Source:** p. 80 (attribution to confirm).

### KAW-9 Competition slide by audience 🟠
- **Rule:** When the audience is a prospect, map the full competitive landscape (too much beats too little), find out beforehand which competing product the prospect uses and what problems it gives them, and explain why you are good rather than why rivals are bad. When the audience is a potential partner, the slide is optional and usually better dropped, because it would point the partner to better organizations to work with.
- **Violation tells:** dismissing competitors; a "we have no competition" claim to a prospect; a full competitor map shown to a partner.
- **Source:** pp. 79-80 (attribution to confirm).

## Thresholds and numbers
- Pitch length: about 10 slides, a 20-minute meeting, discussion after each slide (🟠, margin of p. 80; attribution to confirm).
- The inflated market slide: 3 or 4 slides, a total near $50 billion, a 4 to 5 year horizon (🟠, the author's observation, pp. 84-85).
- Mantra length: 3 to 5 words (🔴, stated in the brain note's gloss of p. 24; not clearly the author's number).

## Anti-patterns
- **The $50 billion slide:** analyst totals on a far horizon -> nobody in the room believes them, the presenter included, and they cost credibility.
- **Mechanism-first pitch:** leading with technical performance -> nobody wakes up wanting a better technical score; keep the diagnostic as proof and as a conversation opener, never as the promise (the brain note's corollary).
- **Mystery title slide:** a title that does not say what the company does -> the audience spends the meeting guessing.
- **"Thank you, any questions?":** closing without a requested next step -> the deal stalls.
- **Competitor bashing:** explaining why rivals are bad -> the customer wanted to hear why you are good.
- **Competition slide for a partner:** -> shows them better partners.
- **Slogan as mantra:** an external tagline in place of an internal answer -> the team has no shared compass.
- **Impersonal copy:** "our platform lets companies..." -> the prospect must translate it into a gain, and most will not.

## Limits
- **Narrow coverage:** the brain holds notes on a few pages only (p. 24, pp. 79-80, pp. 84-85); this file is not a full distillation of the book.
- **Uncertain attribution:** the pitch-structure pages (KAW-6 to KAW-9) are attributed to this book only tentatively; the positioning page (KAW-5) comes from an unnamed book.
- **Obvious markets only:** the imagination chain (KAW-2) works only when the product's usefulness is obvious; the author says so.
- **Practitioner advice, no measured outcomes:** the rules come from the author's experience running an early-stage venture fund; the notes carry no data on win rates.
