# La Table: decision frameworks of the greats (multiple originators, synthesis 2026)

**Covers:** survival math and ruin, reversibility and decision speed, market entry and timing, first customers, cost floors and process pruning, control and capital structure. **Use for:** judging whether a bet survives the conditions of the framework invoked to justify it; how fast to decide; how much to stake; whether to take outside money, refuse an offer or automate a process. A framework quoted without its condition of use is a slogan: each law below carries the condition and the case where it kills.
**Provenance:** web research summary (8 research threads, about 200 queries; primary sources where available: essays, shareholder letters, interview transcripts, SEC filings, biographies), consolidated into brain notes that tag each claim FACT, RECONSTRUCTION or LEGEND; notes dated 2026-09-05; synced 2026-10-09.

## Laws

### TAB-1 Default alive or default dead 🟠
- **Rule:** When any bet, hire or spend is on the table, first compute, every month, whether the business reaches profitability before the cash runs out at current expenses and at the revenue growth of the last few months; if the answer is default dead, revenue becomes the only priority, because this is the control framework that bounds every other one and growth comes from product and sales far more than from budget.
- **Violation tells:** nobody can state cash, burn, 3-month revenue growth and the projected profitability date; a bet launched while default dead without anyone saying so; hiring ahead of revenue (Graham names hiring too fast as the top killer of funded startups); tooling or automation work while default dead. It kills when posed too early (it pushes to over-frugality), and it says nothing about what to cut.
- **Cheapest check:** one row per month: cash, burn, revenue growth over the last 3 months, date of profitability or of the empty bank account.
- **Source:** Paul Graham, essay "Default Alive or Default Dead?" (October 2015); Airbnb waited four months after raising before its first hire (cited in the essay).

### TAB-2 Barbell strategy and ruin avoidance 🟠
- **Rule:** When splitting money between a safe activity and a speculative one, keep the large majority ultra-safe and a small share on unlimited upside, never let a stake touch survival cash, never fund the speculative leg with debt on the safe leg, always keep cash for one more attempt, and refuse any deal whose worst case is ruin whatever its expected value, because a risk that can eliminate you becomes certain once repeated over time (ergodicity).
- **Violation tells:** personal savings, credit or the safe business's cash pledged to the moonshot; "the expected value is positive" used to excuse a downside that includes ruin; no reserve for one more try; an exit or a surplus spent on lifestyle instead of funding the next stage. It kills twice: the speculative leg bleeds through long calm periods, and a solo founder cannot barbell time (it sits 100% on one bet); only money can be split.
- **Cheapest check:** write the bet's worst case in cash and compare it with the survival reserve (floor living costs for N months plus one more attempt).
- **Source:** Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile (2012), Skin in the Game (2018) and the essay "The Logic of Risk Taking" (2017). The application to founders is the research's extension, Taleb writes about trading and life. One-more-shot case: SpaceX had cash for a single launch when the fourth Falcon 1 flight succeeded (September 2008); Beal Aerospace, Rotary Rocket and Kistler died on the same bet.

### TAB-3 One-way and two-way doors (Type 1 and Type 2 decisions) 🟠
- **Rule:** Before deliberating, classify the decision as reversible or not at your own cash scale; reversible (a tool, a test price, a content angle) means decide within hours with about 70% of the information you want; irreversible (a lease, a partner, debt, a client exclusivity) means slow down, because heavy process applied to reversible decisions produces slowness, timid risk aversion and too few experiments.
- **Violation tells:** days of deliberation over a tool or a test price; a bet called "two-way" by pointing at a large company when its failure would empty the founder's treasury. It kills when reversibility is read from the nature of the decision instead of from the treasury: the Fire Phone write-off was a two-way door for Amazon, the same failure is a one-way door for a solo founder.
- **Cheapest check:** ask what undoing the decision costs, in cash and in months, as a share of the reserve.
- **Source:** Jeff Bezos, Amazon 2015 letter to shareholders (published April 2016); the 70% rule is in the 2016 letter; Fire Phone loss reported by GeekWire and Time (October 2014). This is a Bezos framework: nothing in the sources shows Zuckerberg formulating it.

### TAB-4 Start small and monopolize, with a falsification date 🟠
- **Rule:** When an idea rests on a contrarian secret and on a small market to dominate first, write down the segment, the single channel and a dated falsification test ("if fewer than N paying customers by date D, the secret is false"), because conviction without a market correction mechanism destroys capital: a secret is an asset only if the market proves it right within your survival horizon.
- **Violation tells:** a "nobody gets it yet" thesis with no deadline; deadlines moved each time they pass; a micro-market that cannot be named in one line. It kills as contrarianism without a falsification date: Thiel's own hedge fund, Clarium Capital, fell from a peak above $8B of assets in 2008 to about $350M in 2011, down about 90%, after three losing years.
- **Cheapest check:** the written falsification line (segment, channel, number of paying customers, date), checked on that date without moving it.
- **Source:** Peter Thiel, "Competition Is for Losers" (Wall Street Journal, September 2014), adapted from Zero to One (2014), whose base rule the THI file covers. La Table adds only the Clarium guard (Bloomberg, January 2011) and the falsification date.

### TAB-5 Do things that don't scale 🟠
- **Rule:** In the phase from 0 to about 20 customers, recruit and onboard users by hand, in the "Collison installation" form (set the product up on the prospect's machine on the spot instead of sending a link), and require each manual act to produce either a paying customer or a reusable playbook step, because direct contact with the earliest users gives the best feedback you will ever get and the manual phase must stay temporary.
- **Violation tells:** waiting for users to arrive; a launch event or a partnership expected to create the first traction; the same manual work repeated customer after customer with nothing written down. It kills when the manual acts never feed a scalable loop: consulting disguised as a startup. Automating that consulting with software agents multiplies the trap instead of fixing it.
- **Cheapest check:** after 10 hand-made onboardings, count how many paid and how many playbook steps got written.
- **Source:** Paul Graham, essay "Do Things that Don't Scale" (July 2013); Airbnb's door-to-door apartment photos in New York (2009 to 2010) and the Collison brothers at Stripe, both told by Graham and confirmed by the founders.

### TAB-6 First principles 🟠
- **Rule:** When an industry cost is presented as fixed, replace the historical price with the physical floor of the components and attack the gap, because reasoning by analogy freezes past prices into false laws of nature.
- **Violation tells:** the method applied to customer behavior, human dexterity, regulation or delivery dates. It kills when the binding constraint is human or regulatory instead of physical: the over-automated Model 3 line in 2018, which Musk called his own mistake ("Humans are underrated"), and self-driving promised for "next year" since 2016.
- **Cheapest check:** a one-page decomposition: each component's market cost next to its theoretical minimum; the gap is either the opportunity or the illusion.
- **Source:** Elon Musk, interview with Kevin Rose (September 2012), battery case; Bloomberg (April 2018) for the automation reversal.

### TAB-7 The algorithm (Musk's 5 steps) 🟠
- **Rule:** Before tooling any process, run five steps in order: question every requirement (each one carries the name of a person, never of a department), delete every part or step you can, simplify and optimize, accelerate the cycle, and automate last, because automating a process nobody pruned locks the waste in, the error Musk admits making himself.
- **Violation tells:** an automation or AI-agent layer built over a process nobody pruned; requirements owned by "the team" or "legal" with no name; optimizing before deleting; nothing ever added back after a deletion (the sign that deletion was too timid). It kills whenever step 5 comes first.
- **Cheapest check:** for one pipeline, list each step with an owner's name, strike what can go, and automate only what survives.
- **Source:** Elon Musk as reported by Walter Isaacson, Elon Musk (2023), read through secondary summaries.

### TAB-8 The probability written once 🟠
- **Rule:** When committing to a long, uncertain bet, write down its probability of success once, decide whether the payoff justifies the stake, then execute without reopening the decision every week; revise only on a hard signal, because weekly re-deliberation exhausts the founder and adds no information.
- **Violation tells:** the same go/no-go reopened weekly with no new data; no written probability at all. It kills in two cases: the probability is never revised after hard signals arrive, or the stake touches survival cash (then TAB-2 vetoes the bet).
- **Cheapest check:** one written line: probability, payoff, stake, and the signal that would trigger a review; check when it was last revised and why.
- **Source:** Elon Musk, own post (January 2024) giving his starting estimate for SpaceX and Tesla; a retrospective account written two decades after the bets.

### TAB-9 The future is not in the price, plus the structural analogy 🟠
- **Rule:** When refusing an offer or making a large allocation bet, decide on the product roadmap only you can see, backed by compared retention metrics and by a historically identical precedent, because an acquirer prices the present and a structurally identical past case is the best forecast available.
- **Violation tells:** an acquisition offer refused with no unpriced product pipeline, no retention edge and no network effects; no control structure able to survive the dissent (Facebook's management team left within a year of the Yahoo refusal). It kills without structural control or a real pipeline: the same gesture killed Friendster, which refused about $30M from Google in 2003 and died.
- **Cheapest check:** name the precedent and the retention numbers (day 1, 7, 30 return rates) that justify the bet; if neither exists, the refusal is a roulette spin.
- **Source:** Mark Zuckerberg: the $1B Yahoo refusal (2006) as told by Peter Thiel (Forbes video, October 2014); WhatsApp (2014) decided on retention and the Tencent precedent (Forbes); the Llama open-weights letter (July 2024) built on the Unix and Linux precedent. His 2024 statement that the board tried to fire him diverges from Thiel's contemporary account; the hard fact is the management exodus.

### TAB-10 Regret minimization framework 🔴
- **Rule:** When facing an irreversible, long-horizon life decision (leaving a job, emigrating, changing career), project yourself to age 80 and minimize the number of regrets, always bounded by the ruin floor of TAB-2, because the framework ignores probabilities and base rates and a regret does not pay rent.
- **Violation tells:** used for operational or weekly decisions; used to justify a bet whose downside is ruin; no base rate considered. It kills when applied without a ruin floor: the thousands of dot-com leavers who reasoned the same way are never interviewed at 80.
- **Source:** Jeff Bezos, Academy of Achievement interview (May 2001), about leaving D.E. Shaw in 1994. The brain flags the framework as a reconstruction, possibly a legend: the story was told seven years later by a rich man, and it omits about $245K invested by his parents, an employer who let him leave cleanly, and the 1994 internet timing.

### TAB-11 Control before capital 🟠
- **Rule:** Before taking outside capital or a partner, lock control by legal structure (share classes, board seats, vesting, no forced sale or drag-along you cannot block) and read the full waterfall with the name of a person behind every clause, because the right to be wrong is secured before taking the bets, never after: control is bought in advance.
- **Violation tells:** "we trust each other" standing in for terms; a term sheet judged on its headline valuation; liquidation preferences and drag-along never modeled at a modest exit; an investor or associate able to force a sale or a pivot. Counter-proofs: Musk was removed as CEO of the merged X.com and Confinity (later PayPal) by a board vote in 2000; Scale AI's founder obtained voting control only as a clause of the Meta deal; FanDuel's founders received nothing from a $465M sale because of preferences and drag-along rights.
- **Cheapest check:** model the waterfall at a low, a mid and a high exit value and read what common shares receive in each.
- **Source:** Facebook's dual-class recapitalization (November 2009) and 2012 IPO prospectus (SEC 424(b)(4)): the refusal of Yahoo, the $19B WhatsApp purchase and two years burned on HTML5 were survivable because the founder could not be fired. Noam Wasserman, "The Founder's Dilemma" (Harvard Business Review, February 2008). FanDuel sale (2018) from press and the founders' lawsuit.

### TAB-12 Timing first: position on what is opening 🟠
- **Rule:** When choosing a market, pick the one a platform shift is opening and whose buyers' spending rises steeply, then stay in play at minimal survival cost until the window opens, because timing weighs more than team or idea in the outcome and cannot be chosen, only positioned for; in the documented cases the idea came after the shift.
- **Violation tells:** a market chosen because the founder likes it; no named shift that resets the counters; a target budget that is flat or shrinking; a burn too high to wait for the window. It kills as the same concept at the wrong time: Z.com died in 1999 and 2000 for lack of broadband, YouTube won in 2005 with the same idea.
- **Cheapest check:** name the shift, and plot the target buyers' budget for this category over the last 12 months: rising or flat.
- **Source:** Bill Gross (Idealab), TED 2015, his own coding of 100+ companies on 5 factors; Carta cohort data; shifts behind Huang (3D consumer graphics, 1993), Koum (App Store and push notifications, 2009) and Zhang Yiming (smartphones, 2012). The spending-curve rule ("choose a spending curve, not a market") comes from AI data vendors that sell to frontier labs whose budgets double within months.

## Thresholds and numbers
- Reversible decision: decide with about 70% of the information you wish you had (🟠, Bezos, 2016 letter).
- Fire Phone: about $170M written off, $83M of it unsold inventory, absorbed in one quarter by a company with about $20B of quarterly revenue (🟠, GeekWire and Time, October 2014).
- Barbell split: about 90% ultra-safe, about 10% speculative (🔴, secondary book notes on Antifragile).
- Batteries: about $600/kWh market price against about $80/kWh of materials at metal spot prices in 2012; packs reached $139/kWh in November 2023, about $103 in 2012 dollars (🟠, Musk interview; price series via a secondary source).
- Deletion test of the algorithm: if you do not add back at least 10% of what you deleted, you did not delete enough (🔴, Isaacson via secondary summary).
- Musk's starting probability for SpaceX and for Tesla: under 10% (🟠, his own post, January 2024, retrospective).
- WhatsApp purchase justified by 70% daily return against 62% for Facebook (🟠, Forbes, 2014).
- Growth of 10% a week turns 100 users into about 14,000 within a year (🟠, Graham, 2013; the arithmetic holds).
- Timing explains about 42% of the gap between success and failure, ahead of team and idea (🟠, Bill Gross, own unaudited coding, TED 2015).
- Seed to Series A within 2 years: 30.6% for the Q1 2018 seed cohort, about 15% for the 2022 cohort (🟠, Carta data, 2024).
- Average US unicorn overvalued by 48%, common shares by 56%; 8 share classes on average; 65 of 135 unicorns lose the status once terms are priced (🟢, Gornall and Strebulaev, Journal of Financial Economics, 2020).
- 50% of founders are no longer CEO by year 3; giving up control comes with an 80 to 100% value premium, the "Rich versus King" trade-off (🟠, Wasserman, HBR 2008, 212 startups).
- Facebook's founder held about 57% of votes at the 2012 IPO with a minority of the equity (🟢, SEC prospectus 424(b)(4)).
- Founding team median equity: 56% after seed, 36% after Series A, 16.1% after Series C, less than the employee option pool (🔴, Carta 2026 report via a secondary summary).
- US QSBS (Section 1202): gain exclusion of $10M ($15M for shares issued after July 4, 2025) or 10 times the basis; plan it, or the local equivalent, at incorporation and never at sale (🟢, public law).
- Base rates: YC acceptance about 0.6 to 1% (2025 to 2026); unicorn probability after a seed round about 0.5 to 2.5% depending on the source; 70 to 85% of seed-funded startups never reach Series A; about 0.00006% of all startups become unicorns (🔴, secondary aggregations that disagree).
- 55% of US unicorns have an immigrant founder, 319 of 582, mostly entering through a tech job or a university (🟠, NFAP, July 2022).
- Employment as a paid school (knowledge, network, credibility, capital): 7 to 11 years of employment, jump between ages 29 and 35 (🔴, pattern drawn from a handful of documented cases).
- Founder mental health: 72% report at least one lifetime mental health condition against 48% of controls; depression 30%, about twice the controls; addiction about 3 times; bipolar disorder 11% (🟢, Freeman et al., 242 entrepreneurs against 93 controls, self-reported, survivors only, so probably a floor; peer-reviewed 2019).
- 81% of founders hide stress and fears, more than half even from their cofounder (🟠, Startup Snapshot survey).
- Investors estimate that 10 to 20% of their portfolio companies have a founder in depression or substance abuse (🟠, investor estimates in the press).
- African tech founders, 160+ across 13 countries: 80%+ report mental well-being difficulties, anxiety 60%, burnout 52%; top stressor is raising capital (59%); 14% feel able to tell their investors (🟠, Flourish Ventures survey, November 2024).
- Gross versus net: one AI talent marketplace's "$2B run rate" is gross; net revenue estimated at about $180M to $250M (🟠, Sacra estimate, 2026).

## Anti-patterns
- **Framework as slogan:** quoting a great's framework to justify a bet without checking its condition of use -> every framework above has a case where it kills, and the quote never carries it.
- **The self-made story:** reading a founder's rise as pure merit -> social sorting precedes market sorting (private tutoring and elite schools, physicist parents, family seed money, about $245K from parents in one case); the starting capital is schooling, network and psychology before money.
- **"He risked everything":** copying the 2008 all-in -> it was a multimillionaire's all-in with access to rescue capital, saved by a $1.6B NASA contract three months after the only successful launch; the transferable lesson is to keep cash for one more shot (TAB-2).
- **A hundred small customers as the survival plan:** -> anchor contracts saved SpaceX (NASA CRS, December 2008) and Tesla (a $465M DOE loan); one to three recurring contractual customers bridge an air pocket better than a crowd of small ones.
- **Consuming the exit:** treating the first exit as a lifestyle -> Musk put Zip2 money into X.com and PayPal money into SpaceX and Tesla; Mo Ibrahim sold MSI before scaling Celtel; exit N is the ammunition for stage N+1.
- **The paper billionaire:** taking a headline valuation for wealth -> mark-to-market on a tiny float, common shares overvalued 56% on average behind 8 share classes; before the exit, only secondaries and dividends are real money (TAB-11).
- **Refusing the offer as the visionary move:** -> Friendster refused Google and died; the Yahoo refusal was rational only combined with an unpriced product pipeline and a control structure that survived the management exodus (TAB-9).
- **Suffering as the cause of success:** 120-hour weeks worn as proof -> the biggest winner of the corpus has no documented physiological sacrifice because structure spared him survival mode; founders sabotage sleep, exercise and food first; suffering is a cost that correlates with the stories only because survivors publish.
- **Paying the price in private:** hiding distress from everyone, cofounder included -> isolation is the aggravating factor; choose in advance one person to tell everything.
- **The exit repairs everything:** deferring health and relationships until the sale -> the hedonic treadmill is documented (a founder sold his company for about $970M and stayed unhappy); any price paid "until the exit" is sunk, and broken marriages, health and friendships are not bought back.
- **Drop out and found:** -> base rates near zero; the documented route for people born outside the network is employment as a paid school, then a jump on a platform shift.
- **The best product wins:** -> Snapchat had the better product and Instagram Stories passed it within a year through distribution; most of the biggest consumer apps used one unpaid distribution strategy at a time (Rachitsky, 2020), and the startup must win distribution before the incumbent wins innovation (Rampell, a16z).
- **ARR proves the business:** -> marketplace gross is not revenue, burning $1 to earn 90 cents is not a business, and part of fast "vertical" AI revenue is the labs' venture money recycled into supplier spend; the number that does not lie is default alive (TAB-1).
- **Venture money as the only path:** -> post-seed unicorn odds sit around 0.5 to 2.5%; bootstrapped Surge AI (zero VC, about 110 employees, over $1B of revenue in 2024) out-earned the VC-backed category leader while its founder kept control; bootstrapping is the route where the median case is not ruin.
- **The dirty shortcut:** buying a license or a deal with a bribe or an offshore arrangement -> Masiyiwa spent five years in constitutional court instead of paying and won his license (1997); Ibrahim required the full board's signature above $30K, walked away from two markets and sold Celtel for $3.4B; a clean company sells at world price, while a compromised source of capital can cost a founder for life (a fund founder whose $1B seed came conditioned on an offshore structure ended with $139M in penalties and a non-prosecution agreement, DOJ 2020).
- **Fighting for the main door:** an outsider competing head-on where origin is a handicap -> the documented outsiders (Ogunlesi, Steward, Ibrahim) entered where their origin was a competence, then left that niche before it became a ceiling.

## Limits
- Survivorship bias runs through the whole library: these frameworks come from winners, and the people who reasoned the same way and vanished write no essays or shareholder letters. Only two entries are guardrails instead of winners' narratives, default alive (TAB-1) and ruin avoidance (TAB-2); they bound all the others.
- Most evidence is self-reported or second-hand: essays, interviews, biographies read through summaries, press. No law in this file reaches 🟢; the 🟢 marks above apply to single numbers (a peer-reviewed study, an SEC filing, a statute).
- Several frameworks were narrated long after the decision: regret minimization (seven years later), Musk's probability (a 2024 post about 2002 to 2004), Zuckerberg's account of the Yahoo board (2024). Treat the narrative as a reconstruction.
- Frameworks forged on large balance sheets do not scale down: Amazon absorbed the Fire Phone in a quarter, and Musk's tolerance for ruin sat above a floor of network, employability and rescue capital that most founders lack.
- Taleb's barbell is about trading and life; its use for founders is an extension, and it does not cover time.
- Timing (TAB-12) is mostly outside the founder's control: Gross's 42% comes from his own unaudited coding, and riding a steep spending curve means customer concentration (one marketplace drew about 90% of its revenue from a handful of labs) on revenue partly made of recycled venture money.
- Numbers about private companies (valuations, ARR, run rates, founders' stakes) come from press and analysts, not audited accounts.
