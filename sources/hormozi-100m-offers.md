# $100M Offers: How to Make Offers So Good People Feel Stupid Saying No (Alex Hormozi, 2021)

**Covers:** offer design, pricing from perceived value, the Value Equation, the Grand Slam Offer, guarantees and risk reversal, perception and status as levers. **Use for:** judging whether an offer can carry a premium price, why a product does not convert, how to package and price, which lever to pull before building more.
**Provenance:** book notes (French edition, pp. 57 and 59) and summary notes of the book, cross-read with Acquisition.com's public offer checklists (pricing and value, offer creation, bonuses, guarantees, scarcity and urgency); synced 2026-10-09. **Deep dive:** `artifacts/100m-offers-summary.html` (interactive summary page, FR).

## Laws

### HOR-1 Market > offer > persuasion 🟠
- **Rule:** When choosing what to sell and to whom, judge the market before the offer and the offer before the copy, because a starving crowd (real pain, money to pay, easy to target, growing) lifts a mediocre offer, while no offer or copy lifts a market without pain or purchasing power. The narrower and more specific the market, the higher the price it can bear.
- **Violation tells:** the idea is described by its product or technology, not by a named buyer; buyers cannot pay or are hard to reach; the plan counts on better copywriting to create demand; buyers can compare it to a free alternative.
- **Cheapest check:** list 10 reachable buyers who have the pain and the budget, and ask 5 of them for a pre-sale before building.
- **Source:** $100M Offers (the hierarchy set before the Grand Slam Offer method).

### HOR-2 Value Equation 🟠
- **Rule:** When setting a price or judging an offer, score it as Value = (dream outcome x perceived likelihood of achievement) / (time delay x effort and sacrifice), and price from that perceived value, never from cost plus margin, because price follows perceived value. Raise the two top terms, crush the two bottom ones. Selling a tool leaves the effort with the buyer and caps the price; delivering the result (done-for-you) crushes the denominator and supports a high price.
- **Violation tells:** cost-plus pricing; the offer lists features instead of an outcome; no proof, case study or guarantee to lift the likelihood; a long wait before the first result; the buyer must learn, set up or change habits to get any value; a commoditized market where the buyer compares to free.
- **Cheapest check:** write each of the four terms in one line, in the buyer's words; the blank or weakest line is the work. Then offer a done-for-you version of the same outcome, at a higher price, to 5 prospects.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Value Equation.

### HOR-3 Grand Slam Offer 🟠
- **Rule:** When building an offer, run the six steps: (1) define the dream outcome with its timing and emotional payoff; (2) list every obstacle, 15 or more; (3) flip each obstacle into a solution; (4) choose a delivery vehicle per solution (format, frequency, intensity); (5) trim and stack: keep the 6 to 10 components of high value and acceptable cost, cut the high-cost low-value ones, prefer one-to-many delivery; (6) add the enhancers: guarantee, scarcity, urgency, bonuses, and a name built with the M-A-G-I-C formula (Magnet, Avatar, Goal, Interval, Container). Do it because an offer built this way sits in a category of one, where price comparison stops.
- **Violation tells:** the offer can be compared line by line with a competitor's; components that cost a lot to deliver and that buyers do not value; marginal cost that grows with each client; no name; only a handful of obstacles identified.
- **Cheapest check:** run steps 1 to 3 on paper, then show the stack to 3 prospects and ask which component they would pay for on its own.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Grand Slam Offer method; Acquisition.com Offer Creation Checklist.

### HOR-4 "Charge more, offer more" 🟠
- **Rule:** When a prospect objects to the price, add value (a bonus, a faster result, less effort, a stronger guarantee) instead of discounting, because a lower price lowers perceived value, and the reverse move (offer less, charge less) does not work.
- **Violation tells:** discount codes as the main conversion lever; "cheaper than X" positioning; the price drops after the first objection.
- **Cheapest check:** at the next price objections, answer with one added bonus at the same price and compare close rates against past discounts.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, pricing principles.

### HOR-5 Guarantee as a price lever 🟠
- **Rule:** When perceived likelihood is the weak term, add a guarantee that reverses risk, because the more risk it moves from buyer to seller, the more the offer can charge. Pick the strongest credible type: unconditional, conditional (tied to the buyer's own execution), anti-guarantee, implied, or service-based (keep working free until an observable improvement). The guarantee must be credible and deliverable.
- **Violation tells:** no guarantee at all; a magic promise the seller cannot honor; a guarantee nobody can realistically invoke.
- **Cheapest check:** write the guarantee in one sentence, then check that the business survives its worst plausible month of claims.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, guarantees; Acquisition.com Unbeatable Guarantee Checklist.

### HOR-6 Perception is reality 🟠
- **Rule:** When improving an offer, change what the buyer perceives (visible progress, visible waiting time, visible result) before paying for the real improvement, because a gain the buyer does not perceive is worth zero to them, and perception levers often cost far less for a larger felt effect. Look for the psychological fix first: logical fixes have usually been tried already, so what remains unsolved is usually psychological.
- **Violation tells:** an engineering roadmap the customer cannot see; progress hidden until delivery; silent waits; speed or quality gains never shown to the buyer.
- **Cheapest check:** add a progress or countdown indicator, or a before/after proof screen, and compare complaints or satisfaction before and after.
- **Source:** $100M Offers (French ed., p. 57): the London Underground countdown map example, and a tip citing Rory Sutherland on premium pricing demanding better marketing than discounting does.

### HOR-7 Status as the real driver of desire 🟠
- **Rule:** When framing the dream outcome, dig under the surface desire to the status gain (the perceived rise relative to others) and describe the result through the eyes of other people, because when two desires compete, the one that raises status usually wins, and many vehicles serve the same underlying desire.
- **Violation tells:** the benefit is a raw metric; the copy talks about the product, never about how peers will see the buyer; "status" read as luxury only, when in the buyer's own group the modest choice can be the one that raises status.
- **Cheapest check:** rewrite the headline from the point of view of the buyer's peers and test it against the feature headline.
- **Source:** $100M Offers (French ed., p. 59): several vehicles for one desire, the minivan versus supercar anecdote, the golf partner tip.

### HOR-8 Get paid to acquire customers 🟠
- **Rule:** When cash limits growth, structure payments so the upfront amount covers the cost of acquiring the customer, and design the offer so one client win repays the price several times, because customer-financed acquisition removes the cash constraint and an obvious return removes the price debate.
- **Violation tells:** acquisition cost paid months before the first payment; monthly-only pricing funded by paid ads; a return the buyer has to compute alone.
- **Cheapest check:** compare the cost to acquire one customer with the cash collected from that customer in the first 30 days.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, as summarized in the book notes (money model principle).

### HOR-9 Bonuses must kill objections 🟠
- **Rule:** When adding a bonus, tie it to one specific objection or to speeding the result (remove a fear, cut effort, raise confidence, accelerate, handle an important edge case) and display its value in the stack, because a decorative bonus adds cost without raising perceived value.
- **Violation tells:** bonuses unrelated to any objection heard in real sales conversations; bonuses with no stated value.
- **Cheapest check:** list the top 3 objections from real conversations; each bonus must map to one of them.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, bonuses; Acquisition.com Bonus Creation Checklist.

### HOR-10 True scarcity and urgency only 🟠
- **Rule:** When adding scarcity or urgency, use only real limits (capacity, cohorts, seats, deadlines, launch price, expiring bonus), because invented scarcity destroys trust once the buyer sees through it.
- **Violation tells:** countdown timers that reset; "3 spots left" with unlimited capacity; a deadline that keeps moving.
- **Source:** $100M Offers, scarcity and urgency; Acquisition.com Scarcity & Urgency Checklist.

## Thresholds and numbers
- List 15 or more obstacles, sorted into 4 categories, before designing solutions (🟠, $100M Offers)
- Keep 6 to 10 high-value components after trim and stack (🟠, $100M Offers)
- Prefer delivery with a high upfront cost and a low marginal cost per client (one-to-many) (🟠, $100M Offers)
- One client win should repay the price several times over (🟠, $100M Offers)
- London Underground: a countdown map costing a few million cut perceived waiting more than faster trains costing several billion would have (🟠, French ed., p. 57; anecdote, no audited figures)
- Acting on perception costs around 1000x less than acting on reality, for a stronger felt effect (🔴, rule of thumb extrapolated in the notes from that single anecdote)

## Anti-patterns
- **Discount to close:** cut the price at the first objection -> lowers perceived value and trains buyers to wait; "charge more, offer more" says the reverse move fails.
- **Feature-first fix:** the product does not convert, so a feature gets added -> it rarely moves outcome, likelihood, delay or effort; ask first what outcome is bought and how to make it more certain, faster, easier and better proven.
- **Selling the tool:** the buyer does the work -> the denominator stays high, the price stays low, and the buyer compares to free tools.
- **Copy to the rescue:** sharper copywriting on a commoditized market -> market sits above offer and persuasion; words cannot lift a weak dream outcome.
- **Decorative bonus:** extras unrelated to any objection -> cost without perceived value.
- **Magic guarantee:** a promise the seller cannot honor -> destroys credibility, then cash when claims arrive.
- **Fake urgency:** resetting timers, invented limits -> trust collapses once seen.
- **Invisible upgrade:** money spent on real improvements the buyer never notices -> zero perceived value.
- **Raw-metric benefit:** the gain stated as a number only -> misses the status payoff of how others will see it.

## Limits
- Every framework comes from the author's own businesses and is stated without controlled study. The Value Equation is a mental model, not a measured formula: its terms and its multiplicative form are not calibrated.
- The author's business results are self-reported; no entity of his portfolio publishes audited accounts.
- The model assumes high-margin services or info-products; its transfer to low-ticket software is unproven.
- On commoditized markets where a free alternative exists, the dream outcome term is weak and offer engineering alone cannot carry a premium price.
- The perception principle sets no floor of real substance; read alone, it can justify cosmetic changes over real delivery.
- The status examples (which desire wins, for whom) are anecdotal and culture-dependent.
