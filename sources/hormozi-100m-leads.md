# $100M Leads: How to Get Strangers to Want to Buy Your Stuff (Alex Hormozi, 2023)

**Covers:** lead magnets, continuity from free to paid, the 4 core advertising channels, the Rule of 100, lead capture benchmarks, misattributed rules. **Use for:** judging a free offer or a funnel, choosing what to give away, diagnosing leads without sales, sizing daily outreach volume, separating real Hormozi rules from imported ones.
**Provenance:** web research summary (2026-10-08: Hormozi's own podcast chapter on lead magnets, cross-checked summaries of the book, third-party conversion benchmarks) plus a sourced frameworks report for the channels and the Rule of 100; no direct reading of the book; synced 2026-10-09.

## Laws

### LDS-1 Lead magnet: a complete solution to a narrowly defined problem 🟠
- **Rule:** When designing a free offer, solve one narrow problem completely, so that solving it reveals a bigger problem the paid offer solves, because continuity from free to paid is set by the scope of the problem, not by withholding information: "complete" stops the magnet from being a teasing ad, "narrow" stops it from swallowing the paid offer. Its job is to turn a contact into an engaged lead, someone who shows interest through an observable act.
- **Violation tells:** free content that teases without solving; a "complete guide to X" that covers the whole paid offer; no logical link between the magnet's problem and the paid offer; success measured in emails captured instead of qualifying acts.
- **Cheapest check:** write the chain in one line: "the magnet solves A; A solved reveals B; the paid offer solves B". If B is not the paid offer's problem, re-cut A.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, lead magnet chapter. "Complete solution for narrowly defined problem" is the chapter title in the author's own podcast version (episode 566), matched by two independent summaries. The book's analogy: free salty snacks at a bar create the thirst the paid drink quenches.

### LDS-2 Advertise the core offer first 🟠
- **Rule:** When starting acquisition, advertise the paid offer directly first, and build a lead magnet only when the core offer is too expensive or too committing to sell cold, because the magnet is a detour, not the default.
- **Violation tells:** a magnet built before the paid offer was ever pitched; a funnel designed before a single direct sale.
- **Cheapest check:** pitch the paid offer directly to 20 contacts; build a magnet only if they decline on cost or commitment.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, per two independent summaries; usually dropped by popular summaries.

### LDS-3 Free value needs a backend that pays for it 🟠
- **Rule:** When a plan relies on giving value away, check that a high-margin paid offer and a human sales and delivery capacity already exist behind it, because the author's own model (books at print cost or free, heavy launch ad spend, a paid ecosystem behind) works only when the backend refinances acquisition; without it, the plan copies the costly half of the model without the profitable half.
- **Violation tells:** a solo founder, a local service or a low-ticket SaaS planning a large free funnel; nobody able to hold a sales conversation; a paid margin that cannot cover the cost of producing and distributing the free piece.
- **Cheapest check:** compare (paid offer margin x expected magnet-to-customer rate) with the cost per magnet delivered.
- **Source:** critique in the research notes, built on the economics of the author's own book launch (self-reported figures).

### LDS-4 The 4 core advertising channels 🟠
- **Rule:** When choosing how to get leads, pick from four channels and four only (warm outreach, free content, cold outreach, paid ads) and name the one in use, because the book treats these four as the only ways to make an offer known.
- **Violation tells:** the plan names no channel; leads are expected to arrive through "virality" or "word of mouth" with no action in any of the four.
- **Cheapest check:** write down the channel and the daily action count for the next two weeks.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, via a sourced summary.

### LDS-5 Rule of 100 🟠
- **Rule:** When running lead generation, perform 100 primary lead-generation actions per day, every day, because acquisition is a daily emission volume on the business side. It is not a follow-up cadence for one prospect; reading it as a nurture rule is a frequent misquote.
- **Violation tells:** outreach done "when there is time"; no daily count; the Rule of 100 used to set touches per prospect.
- **Cheapest check:** log 100 actions a day for 10 working days in one channel, then count engaged leads.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, via a sourced summary. Never validated causally (see Limits).

### LDS-6 Leads without sales means the wrong magnet, not low traffic 🟠
- **Rule:** When a magnet brings leads but no sales, change the problem the magnet solves before touching the ad budget, because zero sales signals a magnet prospects did not want, and a sales problem is not an advertising problem.
- **Violation tells:** ad spend raised after leads fail to buy; traffic blamed while leads exist.
- **Cheapest check:** replace the magnet with a real case study carrying real numbers, and compare bookings at the same spend.
- **Source:** $100M Leads: the author's account of a webinar nobody wanted, replaced by a real gym launch case study with real ad spend and revenue (self-reported).

### LDS-7 Give away the secrets, sell the implementation 🟠
- **Rule:** When afraid the free piece will cannibalize the paid one, do not hold information back; put the paid offer's value in execution (implementation, support, speed, accountability, assets), because information is not the product, execution is, and each problem solved reveals the next one.
- **Violation tells:** content dosed "to keep something for the paid version"; a paid offer that sells only information. Inference drawn in the notes, not stated by the book: an information-only paid offer is structurally exposed to its own magnet.
- **Cheapest check:** list what the buyer gets in the paid offer that is not information; an empty list means this model does not fit.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, attested in two independent sources.

### LDS-8 Quality bar: worth more than your own core offer 🟠
- **Rule:** When judging a magnet's quality, require that it be good enough to charge for and that its free value exceed the price of your own core offer, because reciprocity, reputation (most people who consume it never buy but judge the brand on it) and word of mouth all depend on it; a magnet nobody mentions to others has failed. A costly magnet is fine if it lowers acquisition cost.
- **Violation tells:** a thin checklist nobody would pay for; the bar set against competitors' cheap freebies instead of your own core offer; nobody forwards it.
- **Cheapest check:** ask 5 target buyers whether they would pay for it, and how much.
- **Source:** $100M Leads (author's podcast episode 566 and summaries). The popular rule "give free what competitors charge for" is a practitioner's adaptation that lowers this bar (see Thresholds).

### LDS-9 Optimize for buyer intent, not capture rate 🟠
- **Rule:** When choosing a magnet's format and friction, optimize for the buying intent of the people captured, and add engagement friction rather than remove it, because third-party data show the formats that capture the most bring the least buying intent, and friction at signup raises downstream conversion. This runs against the minimal-friction logic usually attached to free magnets.
- **Violation tells:** format chosen for signup volume; capture rate as the success metric; the ebook as the default format.
- **Cheapest check:** track magnet-to-sales-conversation rate per format, not signups.
- **Source:** third-party benchmarks cited in the research notes (ChartMogul and ProductLed, SaaS conversion report, January 2026, 200 B2B software products; NetLine, B2B content report, about 8 million signups, 2024).

### LDS-10 Three lead magnet types x four delivery vehicles 🟠
- **Rule:** When generating magnet ideas, cross the three types (reveal a problem, sample or trial, one step of a process) with the four vehicles (software, information, service, physical product), because types are defined by their relation to the problem, not by format, and choosing a type chooses the door through which the lead walks to the paid offer. "Solve a problem fully but open a new one" is the cross-cutting principle, not a fourth type.
- **Violation tells:** format picked first; a single mode of consumption (the attested format error is mono-modality, not length); one vehicle treated as superior by rule.
- **Cheapest check:** fill the 3 x 4 grid for one narrow problem and pick the cell the target buyer would act on today.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, via convergent summaries. Inference drawn in the notes, not stated by the book: for a service or implementation offer, type 1 (reveal a problem) aligns continuity and targeting best, because it produces personalized proof of the problem.

### LDS-11 Seven-step lead magnet creation 🟠
- **Rule:** When building a magnet, follow the order: (1) identify the problem and for whom; (2) decide how to solve it (the type); (3) pick the delivery vehicle; (4) test the name; (5) make it easy to consume; (6) make it really good; (7) make it easy to ask for more. Test the name before investing in quality, because the order validates demand before paying the production cost. Aim for less consumption time than the lead expects, because unfinished content gives no result, and no result gives no purchase.
- **Violation tells:** a long asset produced before its title was tested; no clear way to ask for more; content too long to finish.
- **Cheapest check:** run 2 or 3 candidate names as post or ad headlines and compare clicks before producing anything.
- **Source:** $100M Leads, same order in three independent summaries. An unofficial wiki lists nine steps, so the count is not settled.

### LDS-12 Pick the problem only future buyers have 🔴
- **Rule:** When choosing which problem the magnet solves, start from the paid offer: list the obstacles between the buyer and the dream outcome (the Grand Slam Offer inventory), keep painkillers over vitamins and problems that people with money will pay to fix, then pick the obstacle only future buyers experience, because narrowness then works as targeting. Wrong audience and missing logical link are the same error seen from two sides.
- **Violation tells:** the magnet attracts people who will never have the paid offer's problem; the topic was chosen for audience size. Example: a seven-day meal plan for busy parents targets the future personal-training buyer; a complete fitness guide targets nobody.
- **Cheapest check:** ask the last 10 people who consumed the magnet whether they have the problem the paid offer solves.
- **Source:** a composition of $100M Offers (obstacle inventory) and $100M Leads (selection criteria) made by summarizers; not a procedure stated in $100M Leads, hence to test.

## Thresholds and numbers
- Up to 12 distinct magnets per narrow problem (3 types x 4 vehicles) (🟠, $100M Leads via summaries)
- 7 creation steps; an unofficial wiki lists 9 (🟠, count not settled)
- 100 primary lead-generation actions per day, every day (🟠, $100M Leads; never validated causally)
- Failed webinar: $150 a day in ads, 80 leads, 0 sales in 3 days (🟠, the author's self-reported anecdote)
- Book launch: more than 2.9 million copies in one day (2025-08-16), books at print cost or free, more than $4 million of ads before launch (🟠, self-reported)
- 2 to 5% form-fill rate per site visitor (🟠, convergence of three independent vendor sources)
- Median landing page conversion 6.6%, over 41,000 pages and 57 million conversions (🟠, Unbounce benchmark)
- Free to paid: median 8% over six months across 200 B2B software products; trials requiring a credit card convert 25 to 35% at the median, more than 5x no-card trials (🟠, ChartMogul and ProductLed, January 2026)
- Ebooks: 53% of gated content demand, about 983 signups per asset against about 60 for a white paper, but their registrants are 12% less likely to report a purchase decision within 12 months; playbook registrants are 115% more likely (🟠, NetLine, about 8 million signups, 2024)
- Interactive vs static magnets: 26.44% vs 23.22% form conversion, about 14% relative, not the 5x to 6x aggregators repeat (🟠, MailerLite, over 41,000 forms)
- Gated signups up 27% in a year and 84% since 2019; delay between signup and actual opening up from 31 hours (2023) to 39 hours (2024); about 66% of registrants place their purchase horizon beyond one year (🟠, B2B content data cited in the notes)
- Under 1% from download to paying customer (0.67% for B2B SaaS and marketing agencies, 0.81% for professional firms), about 125 to 150 downloads per customer, so 0.02% to 0.1% from visitor to customer (🔴, agency source with no sample or method; order of magnitude only)
- Zero-click searches: 58.5% of US Google searches in 2024, 68% in early 2026; click-through drop near 60% where AI overviews appear (🔴, secondary figures, no primary study in the notes)
- About 15% of form fills qualifiable, so about 85% of triage time unproductive (🔴, single documented case)
- Selective gating: qualified-lead-to-opportunity rate from 9% to 18% in two quarters, at lower volume (🔴, single longitudinal case)
- Do not reuse as data (🔴, self-reported or unsourced): about $200 million yearly revenue, more than 20,000 leads a day, a $46 million exit (self-reported, no audit); acquisition cost cut 3x ($3,000 to $1,000 per customer with a magnet), an arithmetic demonstration with unjustified inputs, not a measurement; headlines multiplying engagement 2x, 3x or 10x and uptake 2x to 4x (no method); click-through from 30% to 70% by rewording (automated video summary, not cross-checked, implausible for an ad); "73% of B2B buyers avoid gated content" attributed to the Content Marketing Institute (the verified CMI report does not mention gating); gated ebooks under 0.9% vs interactive over 5.2% attributed to HubSpot (no primary study found)
- Misattributed, not Hormozi (🔴): "give away free what competitors charge for" (a practitioner's adaptation; the book's bar is your own core offer's price); the hierarchy "tools > services > information" (a single practitioner; the summaries treat the four vehicles as equal and combinable); the "value ladder" (Russell Brunson's term); 5 to 7 follow-up emails (practitioners' number); 80 cents of each ad dollar riding on the headline (the author credits David Ogilvy); persistent follow-up (formalized by Dan Kennedy long before); "whoever can spend the most to acquire a customer wins" (Dan Kennedy, popularized by Brunson, then Hormozi); the origins of "tripwire" and "lead magnet" (not established)

## Anti-patterns
- **Teaser magnet:** free content that withholds the answer -> frustrates, kills reciprocity and reputation.
- **Wide magnet:** "the complete guide to X" -> swallows the paid offer or attracts nobody in particular; cannibalization is a scoping error, not excess generosity.
- **Dosing instead of scoping:** holding information back to protect the paid tier -> the fix is a narrower problem, not less content.
- **Magnet by default:** building a magnet before testing the direct offer -> a detour taken without need.
- **Buying traffic to fix zero sales:** more ads on a magnet nobody buys after -> a sales problem treated as an advertising problem.
- **Ebook for volume:** the highest-capture format -> the lowest buying intent.
- **Free funnel without a backend:** giving value with no high-margin offer behind it -> the costly half of the model without the profitable half.
- **Rule of 100 as a nurture rule:** 100 touches to one prospect -> misreads a daily emission volume.
- **Imported rules sold as Hormozi's:** practitioner benchmarks applied as the book's -> lower bars and wrong cadences.
- **Mono-modal magnet:** one way to consume it -> the format error the summaries actually attest.

## Limits
- No direct reading of the book backs this file: it rests on the author's own podcast chapter and secondary summaries; the number of creation steps is not settled.
- Every Hormozi figure is self-reported, no entity of his portfolio publishes accounts, and the secondary literature loops back to a single source. No academic, investigative or regulatory critique of the model exists.
- The model presupposes paid value that is operational, not informational, a high-margin backend that refinances acquisition, and an existing human sales and delivery capacity. It is weak for solo founders, local services and low-ticket SaaS whose unit economics cannot fund a sales conversation.
- The book's follow-up chapter is undocumented in every summary consulted (no touch count, interval, channel or script); any follow-up cadence presented as Hormozi's is imported.
- The price gap between free and paid is treated nowhere. Brunson's low-priced intermediate step adds a function the free magnet lacks: qualification by payment.
- Nobody measures the real consumption rate of magnets, yet the whole chain (consumed, then result, then purchase) rests on that first link.
- No benchmark exists for local services or for non-English markets, and no third-party data on automated audits as a magnet format; the corpus is B2B tech and SaaS.
- Free vs gated has no general empirical answer: the two available A/B tests contradict each other.
- Third-party benchmarks come mostly from tool vendors and agencies with incompatible bases (form fill per visitor vs conversion per form opened).
- The best-documented degradation sits upstream of the magnet, in distribution (zero-click search), not in magnet design.
- The Rule of 100 is a volume heuristic never validated causally; it assumes info-product or service margins, and applied as-is to low-ticket SaaS it burns cash.
